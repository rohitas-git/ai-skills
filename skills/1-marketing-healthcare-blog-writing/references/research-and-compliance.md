# Healthcare blog research, compliance, drafting, and QA

Use this guide for every healthcare-blog task. It is a drafting control, not medical or legal approval.
Rules change by product, claim, channel, and jurisdiction; verify current primary guidance for the target
market before relying on a regulatory statement.

## 1. Intake and stop conditions

Pin what materially changes the draft:

- reader and situation, including likely anxiety, health-literacy needs, and language;
- article question, search intent, funnel stage, and desired next action;
- product type: general wellness, food, supplement, cosmetic, OTC drug, prescription drug, device,
  diagnostic, or health service;
- target jurisdictions and whether the article is independent education, product-linked promotion, or both;
- exact product facts, approved labeling/instructions, substantiated claims, contraindications, and brand voice;
- freshness cutoff, publication location, review owners, and whether citations must follow a house style.

Stop or constrain the draft when:

- the input contains patient/customer health data, a recognizable story, or free text that may identify a
  person; do not copy it into research tools, and request approved de-identification or synthetic facts;
- the user requests diagnosis, personalized treatment, a dose for a named person, a recommendation to stop
  care, or instructions that could delay urgent treatment;
- the promoted use falls outside approved labeling or instructions, the product category is unknown, or the
  applicable regulator cannot be identified;
- a load-bearing claim lacks evidence relevant to the exact product or the full evidence base is materially
  conflicting; omit the claim or state only the supported uncertainty.

## 2. Evidence workflow

### Source order

1. Current regulator-approved labeling, instructions for use, safety communications, and regulator guidance.
2. Current clinical guidelines or consensus statements from accountable professional/public-health bodies.
3. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses that fit the question.
4. Directly relevant, well-designed human studies; use preclinical evidence only with an explicit boundary.
5. Reputable medical institutions for orientation and patient-friendly explanations—not as a substitute for
   the underlying evidence needed to substantiate a product claim.

Prefer original sources over summaries. Record title, publisher, date, URL or DOI, access date, study type,
population, intervention/product, comparator, outcome, timeframe, limitations, and conflicts of interest.
Do not cite an abstract or press release as if it proves more than the full study.

### Evidence-fit test

For every consequential claim, check:

- **Identity:** Is the evidence about this exact product, or only an ingredient/category?
- **Use:** Do dose, formulation, route, frequency, and duration match?
- **People:** Does the studied population match the intended audience, including age and relevant conditions?
- **Outcome:** Is the measured endpoint clinically meaningful, or merely a surrogate/biomarker?
- **Magnitude:** Are absolute effects and uncertainty available? Avoid relative-risk-only framing.
- **Context:** Does the claim reflect the total reliable evidence, including null or conflicting findings?
- **Freshness:** Could guidance, warnings, recalls, approvals, or consensus have changed?

Never extrapolate an ingredient study to a finished product, an adult study to children or pregnancy, a
biomarker change to a clinical benefit, association to causation, or “no evidence of harm” to “proven safe.”

### AI-assisted workflow

Use AI to accelerate topic angles, question variants, outlines, and first-pass plain-language summaries from
an approved source packet. Do not treat model output as evidence or let it invent emotional context on behalf
of patients. A human editor must remove generic filler, check whether the article responds sensitively to the
reader's actual concern, and restore the brand's real voice; the medical and compliance gates remain unchanged.

### Claims ledger

Create this before final prose:

| ID | Exact express or implied claim | Type | Audience/product | Source and date | Evidence fit and limits | Regulatory status | Disposition |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | … | education / safety / efficacy / comparison / testimonial / approval | … | … | … | verified / review needed / prohibited | keep / revise / remove |

Read the page as a whole. Product name, headline, nearby image, testimonial, study link, CTA, and omitted risk
can combine into a stronger claim than any sentence makes alone. Assess that **net impression** explicitly.

## 3. Regulatory baseline

### United States

- A brand-owned “educational” blog can still function as advertising or labeling when it promotes a product.
  Classify the full context and net impression; do not assume the blog format creates an editorial exemption.
- FTC advertising standards cover express and implied health claims and focus on what reasonable consumers
  take from the full ad. Objective health claims need adequate prior substantiation; evidence must fit the
  advertised product, population, outcome, and use. Testimonials and third-party studies cannot convey claims
  the advertiser could not substantiate directly.
- FDA rules differ by product class. For supplements, distinguish authorized/qualified health claims,
  nutrient-content claims, structure/function claims, and disease claims. A structure/function disclaimer is
  not permission to make an unsubstantiated claim and does not neutralize an otherwise deceptive ad.
- Prescription-drug and restricted-device promotion can require approved-use alignment, material risk
  disclosure, and fair balance. Do not improvise this content; require the current approved labeling and
  specialist medical/regulatory review.
- “FDA approved,” “clinically proven,” “doctor recommended,” “safe,” “natural,” “best,” and comparative claims
  are high-risk. Use them only when the exact wording and substantiation have been verified for the product and
  market.

For other markets, identify the local medicines/device regulator, advertising authority, privacy regime, and
professional rules. Do not transpose US disclaimers or claim categories as universal law.

### Disclaimer and disclosure placement

Include a plain educational disclaimer stating that the article is general information and not a substitute
for professional diagnosis or treatment. Also disclose material commercial relationships and distinguish
editorial education from product promotion. Put qualifications where readers will notice them, near the claim
they qualify. Never rely on a footer disclaimer to fix a misleading headline or net impression.

## 4. Reader-centered drafting

- Lead with what the reader needs to know or do. Acknowledge concern without dramatizing symptoms or promising
  reassurance the evidence cannot support.
- Prefer everyday words and active voice. Define a necessary term immediately: “Bioavailability means how much
  of a substance reaches your bloodstream.” Keep the accurate medical term when readers may need it elsewhere.
- Use short paragraphs, descriptive headings, consistent denominators/timeframes, absolute numbers when
  available, and tables only for genuinely comparable items.
- Separate “what evidence suggests,” “who it may apply to,” “risks and limits,” and “when to talk with a
  clinician.” Do not imply that lack of certainty means two positions are equally supported.
- Use inclusive, non-stigmatizing language. Avoid assuming sex, gender, race, disability, family structure,
  income, or access to care. Include underrepresented populations only when evidence supports the statement;
  otherwise name the evidence gap.
- Stories must be consented and privacy-approved, composite/synthetic and clearly labeled, or omitted. Do not
  imply that one experience predicts typical results.

## 5. SEO and answer-ready structure

Optimize for the reader's intent, not a keyword quota:

- choose one primary query and a small set of natural related questions from supplied or current research;
- make the title and H1 accurate, specific, and non-sensational;
- when a section asks a clear question, give a self-contained answer in its opening roughly 50–100 words,
  followed by evidence, limits, and context;
- use a logical H2/H3 hierarchy, concise lists, comparison tables with identical criteria, descriptive link
  text, and verified internal links;
- provide author/reviewer attribution, relevant credentials, review date, sources, correction path, and
  commercial disclosures when the publisher supports them; never fabricate these trust signals;
- include FAQs only for genuine reader needs already answered in the article. Do not repeat keywords or invent
  thin Q&A solely for search extraction.

Current Google guidance says ordinary SEO and people-first content remain the foundation for generative search.
There is no special AI schema, mandatory content “chunking,” ideal length, or guaranteed citation format.
Structured data must match visible content and its current feature rules; FAQ rich-result eligibility is limited
and should not be promised. Route implementation or sitewide assessment to the existing SEO/schema skills.

### Conversion

Keep the CTA proportionate to the evidence and reader state. Suitable actions include reading an approved
product page, comparing verified specifications, asking a pharmacist/clinician, or booking a consultation.
Avoid urgency, shame, fear, false scarcity, diagnostic quizzes, or language implying the product is the only
safe choice. A CTA must not outrun the article's claims or encourage delaying appropriate care.

## 6. Final QA and sign-off

Run these passes separately:

1. **Clinical:** every medical statement is accurate, current, appropriately qualified, and consistent with
   established consensus or transparently labeled uncertainty.
2. **Claims/compliance:** express and implied claims are in the ledger; evidence predates dissemination; risks,
   limitations, endorsements, and material connections are clear; wording fits the product and jurisdiction.
3. **Privacy:** no personal or re-identifiable health information, hidden metadata, or unapproved real story.
4. **Reader safety:** no diagnosis/prescribing, delayed-care cue, alarmism, false reassurance, or missing material
   risk; urgent-care language, if needed, comes from a current authoritative source.
5. **Plain language:** main point first, jargon defined, one idea per sentence where practical, scannable chunks,
   usable numbers, and no loss of scientific meaning.
6. **Inclusion:** people-first, non-stereotyping language; populations and limitations represented accurately;
   imagery brief avoids tokenism and misleading clinical scenarios.
7. **SEO/AEO:** intent answered, headings descriptive, citations and links valid, no keyword stuffing, no search
   guarantees, and no unsupported schema recommendation.
8. **Editorial:** brand voice feels human; repetition and generic AI filler removed; CTA is useful and restrained.

Final status is **READY TO PUBLISH** only after the real medical, editorial, and required compliance reviewers
are named, their changes are incorporated, and approval is recorded outside the model. Otherwise return
**NOT READY TO PUBLISH** with blockers and owners.

## 7. Primary source notes

Verified 2026-10-24. Recheck these and the target jurisdiction's current rules during live work.

- [FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance): express/implied claims, net impression, prior substantiation, evidence fit, testimonials, and disclosures.
- [FDA Structure/Function Claims](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-food-labeling-and-critical-foods/structurefunction-claims): US supplement claim categories, substantiation, notification, and disclaimer limits.
- [FDA Prescription Drug Advertising and Promotional Labeling](https://www.fda.gov/drugs/surveillance-post-drug-approval-activities/prescription-drug-advertising-and-promotional-labeling): truthful, balanced, accurate promotion.
- [FDA Presenting Risk Information in Prescription Drug and Medical Device Promotion](https://www.fda.gov/media/76269/download): material risk disclosure and fair-balance principles for regulated promotion.
- [HHS HIPAA De-identification Guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/de-identification/index.html): PHI boundaries, Safe Harbor, Expert Determination, and residual re-identification risk.
- [CDC Plain Language Materials & Resources](https://www.cdc.gov/health-literacy/php/develop-materials/plain-language.html): audience-first organization, common words, concise sentences, headings, lists, and testing.
- [CDC Health Literacy Guidance & Tools](https://www.cdc.gov/health-literacy/php/develop-materials/guidance-standards.html): chunking, headings, visuals, and risk-number communication.
- [AHRQ Health Literacy Universal Precautions Toolkit](https://www.ahrq.gov/health-literacy/improve/precautions/index.html): make health information understandable and actionable for people at all health-literacy levels.
- [Google: Creating Helpful, Reliable, People-First Content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content): YMYL accuracy, expert consensus, trust, and the limits of treating E-E-A-T as a single ranking factor.
- [Google: Generative AI Features and Your Website](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/ai-optimization-guide): foundational SEO, people-first value, no special AI schema, no mandatory chunking, and no ideal length.
- [Google: Generative AI Content Guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content): quality, accuracy, context about automation, and e-commerce AI-content policies.
- [Google: FAQ and HowTo Rich Result Changes](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2023/08/howto-faq-changes): limited FAQ rich-result visibility and deprecated HowTo search appearance.
